Style proofs: chapter openings, epigraphs, tags & references

Four decisions to approve before the translation resumes. Specimens are set in the actual Kindle font stack, so what you approve is what renders.

The print design carries all four distinctions in one colour: blue. Chapter numerals, margin tags, citations and epigraphs are all #0000fc and nothing else sets them apart.

On a Paperwhite that blue is a single mid-grey. Every option below therefore carries its distinction in weight, case, rule or indent first, with colour as a bonus on Colorsoft, tablets and the app. Toggle E-ink to judge them the way most reading will happen.

C

Tags — the words in the margin

You're right that these aren't subheadings. In the print they are index anchors: a blue keyword in the left margin marking the line where a term is defined, and the Subject Index at the back points back to exactly those pages. There are 3,843 of them.

The template currently renders them as small-caps mini-headings above the paragraph, which reads as a subtitle and breaks the flow. Four ways to say “this term is defined here” instead.

D

Citations & bibliography

The PDF carries 3,788 citation links as named destinations — cite.Eliza, cite.j:weiz — each pointing at its bibliography entry. That means citations can be resolved exactly, by BibTeX key, with no regex guessing. Right now every one of them is flat text.

D1Faithful link approved matches print invisible in grayscale

…by imitating the responses of a Rogerian psychotherapist (Weizenbaum, 1966). As a result, this work led researchers to first think about the impacts of chatbots on their users (Weizenbaum, 1976).

Exactly what the print does — and toggle E-ink to see the problem: colour alone leaves no trace on grey. Still tappable, just not visible as a link.

D2Small-caps author survives grayscale scholarly register

…by imitating the responses of a Rogerian psychotherapist (Weizenbaum, 1966). As a result, this work led researchers to first think about the impacts of chatbots on their users (Weizenbaum, 1976).

Author in small caps, year carrying the colour. The citation stays legible as a citation on e-ink and gains a second cue on colour devices. Caveat: font-variant: small-caps is honoured unevenly across Kindle firmware — the fallback is plain text, which is acceptable.

D3Superscript marker frees the measure loses author at a glance

…by imitating the responses of a Rogerian psychotherapist.[1] As a result, this work led researchers to first think about the impacts of chatbots on their users.[2]

Pulls the parenthetical out of the sentence entirely — noticeably better rhythm at 6″, where “(Weizenbaum, 1966)” often costs a whole line. But this is a textbook: knowing it's Weizenbaum without tapping is part of reading it.

Found while measuring — these need fixing regardless of which styles you pick

  • The chapter numeral is being dropped. It's in the text layer as a 60 pt blue glyph; the extractor keeps “CHAPTER” as a paragraph and discards the “2”.
  • The epigraph attribution leaks into the prose. Chapter 2's first body block literally starts “Weizenbaum (1966) The dialogue above is from ELIZA…”.
  • Multi-word tags are split. “regular expressions” arrives as two separate margin blocks; so does “inflectional morphemes”.
  • The margin classifier over-captures. Book-wide it yields 3,843 blocks, but on the index and bibliography pages it is picking up entries like “$ (re end-of-line), 21” as margin notes.
  • Cross-references beyond citations are also live in the PDF: 520 figure, 314 section and 175 equation destinations, all currently flat text.
